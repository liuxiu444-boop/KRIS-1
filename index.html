Learning to Design for Belonging

From my experience as an ESL student to my development as a future inclusive educator.

I am Kris Liu, a student in the Master of Arts in Educational Studies (Inclusive Education) program at Trinity Western University. My experience as an ESL student is one of the reasons I chose this program. I value the support ESL programs provide, and I want to explore how they can respond more closely to each learner’s background, strengths, and needs.

Through MAES, I am learning to connect educational theory with thoughtful curriculum planning. My goal is to help English language learners develop academic confidence, participate in their classrooms, and feel that they belong.

My Journey

From individual support to inclusive design

The MAES program gradually changed the way I think about inclusion. Different courses gave me different lenses: research, assessment, neurodiversity, behaviour, language, curriculum design, ethics, and UDL. The common thread became clearer over time — inclusion is not one strategy or one accommodation, but a way of thinking about how learning environments are designed.

1

Beginning with questions

Early coursework pushed me to think more carefully about culture, diversity, research, and whose experiences are centered in education.

2

Understanding learner variability

Courses on cognitive, behavioural, and assessment needs helped me see that a score, diagnosis, or observable difficulty never tells the whole story of a learner.

3

Connecting language and access

Work on multilingual learners and academic language made me more aware that students can understand content while still being blocked by the way information is presented or responses are required.

4

Moving toward design

UDL coursework and my proposed capstone direction are bringing these ideas together: design the environment more flexibly from the beginning, then use assessment and reflection to refine support.

My central learning: Inclusive education is not about making learning easier. It is about removing barriers that are unrelated to the actual learning goal, while preserving challenge, dignity, participation, and high expectations.

Professional Identity & Development

My developing professional identity

My commitment to inclusive education begins with lived experience and grows through research, reflection, and curriculum planning.

Why I chose MAES

As a former ESL student, I understand that learning in a new language also means navigating unfamiliar classroom expectations and adjusting to life in a new country. ESL programs offer valuable support. I joined MAES because I wanted to understand how education could build on that support and respond more thoughtfully to individual differences.

My teaching philosophy

As a future educator, I want to integrate scaffolding and Universal Design for Learning into curriculum planning. I aim to provide clear explanations, model unfamiliar tasks, and offer different ways for students to demonstrate understanding. I want to maintain meaningful learning goals while making the routes toward those goals more accessible.

Connecting research with curriculum

My literature synthesis has helped me consider how visuals, models, discussion, and multilingual resources can support access to academic content. It also prompts me to plan different ways for students to communicate understanding and participate with peers. In future teaching, I want to connect these options with clear learning goals and explicit language scaffolding.

Belonging in a new environment

I hope to help ESL students understand Canadian classroom expectations, build confidence, and connect with their peers. Supporting adjustment should also mean valuing the languages, cultures, and experiences students bring. My own educational experiences in China and Canada motivate me to work toward that balance.

My next step: Develop curriculum that combines clear scaffolds with flexible participation and response options. I want to use student feedback and evidence of understanding to refine support, rather than assume one approach works for everyone.

Research Project Overview

Universal Design for Learning and K-12 English language learners

Proposed capstone direction. I have not yet started my capstone. My EDUC 697A literature synthesis is preliminary coursework that is helping me develop this research focus.

Research question

How can UDL support the academic learning and classroom participation for K-12 English language learners across different subject areas?

Purpose and focus

I want to explore how flexible curriculum design can support K–12 English language learners in understanding subject content and taking part in classroom learning. My experience as an ESL student motivates this inquiry, while my EDUC 520 project connects it to the practical work of planning language support within subject teaching.

Subquestions

  • How do UDL strategies support the academic learning of K–12 English language learners?
  • How does UDL influence the classroom participation and engagement of K–12 English language learners?

Preliminary approach

My literature synthesis compares UDL reviews and intervention research with studies of multilingual teaching, multimodal learning, and ESL program design. I organize these readings around academic access, participation, and implications for inclusive instruction.

Themes from my literature synthesis

The readings connect academic access with classroom participation. These themes summarize my preparatory literature synthesis and will inform the direction of my capstone.

Academic learning

Multiple ways to understand subject content

Tai (2022), Williams et al. (2019), and Meskill et al. (2019) describe how multilingual resources, visuals, gestures, models, and discussion help English learners make meaning in science, mathematics, and biology. Williams et al. examines Grade 5 learners’ use of speech, writing, drawing, gestures, and models; Meskill et al. focuses on instructional conversations in a sheltered high-school biology classroom.

Bray et al. (2024) connects this theme to UDL implementation: its review found that technology-supported UDL in secondary education often emphasized multiple means of representation, offering different ways to access learning materials.

Implication for curriculum: Plan several routes into the same academic concept, such as an explanation, visual model, demonstration, and discussion. This can reduce language-related barriers while keeping the subject learning goal visible.

Evidence distinction: The multilingual classroom studies illustrate practices aligned with UDL; they are not all direct UDL interventions. Bray et al. examines UDL more broadly, rather than English learners specifically.

Classroom participation

Choice, interaction, and belonging

Katz (2013) reports increased active engagement, peer interaction, autonomy, and inclusion in classrooms using the Three Block Model of UDL. Michalovich (2023) explores how digital multimodal composing supports newcomer students’ participation, identity, and investment in learning. Li (2018) examines how a more inclusive, culturally responsive ESL program in a Greater Vancouver high school can address separation and stigma.

Across these readings, participation involves opportunities to communicate ideas, interact with peers, and feel included in classroom learning. Flexible forms of expression can help students contribute while their English is developing.

Implication for curriculum: Provide purposeful choices in how students communicate and demonstrate understanding, with opportunities for peer interaction and attention to students’ languages and identities.

Evidence distinction: Katz’s study is not specific to English learners. Michalovich and Li inform the participation theme through multilingual education research, rather than direct tests of UDL.

Evidence and implementation

Connecting UDL principles with learning goals

Ok et al. (2017) identifies promising K–12 outcomes for engagement, curriculum access, and learning, while noting inconsistencies in how UDL is implemented and measured. King-Sears et al. (2023) reports an overall positive effect on academic achievement. These reviews support attention to UDL as an instructional framework, while drawing on populations broader than K–12 English learners.

Bray et al. (2024) also shows that access to materials receives more attention than engagement and action and expression. This suggests that a flexible lesson needs to consider how students participate and communicate learning alongside how content is presented.

Implication for my inquiry: Examine which UDL practices are used, who participates in each study, and how academic learning and participation are assessed across subjects. This will help me connect the evidence to specific curriculum decisions.

Evidence distinction: Differences in populations, definitions, technologies, and measures affect how findings can be applied to K–12 English learners.

Understanding barriers to ESL learning

Panhwar et al. (2018) identifies language difficulties, limited confidence, motivation, teaching practices, and curriculum-related concerns as barriers to ESL learning and participation. I use this study as background for understanding the need for responsive support. Its university setting means it does not directly establish outcomes for K–12 learners.

What the synthesis suggests: UDL can guide support through multiple ways to access academic content, demonstrate understanding, and participate with peers. The multilingual classroom studies offer practical examples of these possibilities, while the broader UDL studies help frame questions about learning and engagement.

Next steps: Refine the capstone scope and approach with faculty guidance, examine how the studies address different learners and subject areas, and develop implications for curriculum planning that connects scaffolding, flexible access, and meaningful participation.
